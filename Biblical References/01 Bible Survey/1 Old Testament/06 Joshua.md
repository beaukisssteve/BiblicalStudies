# 06 Joshua — Bible Survey

> [!summary] Central message
> The LORD faithfully gives Israel the land He promised, calls His people to courageous obedience, and demonstrates that covenant blessing rests on His faithfulness rather than human strength.

## Book Information

- **Hebrew name:** יְהוֹשֻׁעַ (*Yehoshua*) — “The LORD is salvation.”
- **Traditional author:** Joshua, with possible later editorial additions (especially the account of his death).
- **Date written:** Traditionally placed near the events of the conquest, approximately the late second millennium BC; the final composition and editing are debated.
- **Historical setting:** Israel enters Canaan after Moses’ death, conquers key strongholds, receives tribal inheritances, and renews the covenant.
- **Key verse:** Joshua 21:45 — Not one of the LORD's good promises to Israel failed.

## Structure

1. **Joshua 1–5:** Preparation and entry into the land.
2. **Joshua 6–12:** Conquest and battles in Canaan.
3. **Joshua 13–21:** Division and inheritance of the land.
4. **Joshua 22–24:** Covenant loyalty and Joshua's farewell.

## Covenants

### Abrahamic Covenant — Land Promise Advanced
- **Promise:** God pledged land to Abraham's descendants (Genesis 12:7; 15:18–21; 17:8).
- **In Joshua:** The land is entered and distributed (Joshua 1:2–6; 11:23; 21:43–45).
- **Significance:** The conquest and allotments demonstrate God's faithfulness, even while some territory remains to be possessed (Joshua 13:1).
- **New Testament connection:** Hebrews 11:8–16 looks beyond earthly inheritance to a better, heavenly country.

### Mosaic Covenant — Obedience, Blessing, and Judgment
- **In Joshua:** Meditation on the law (1:7–8), covenant signs (5:2–12), Achan's judgment (7), covenant reading (8:30–35), and renewed allegiance (24).
- **Significance:** Israel's possession of the land does not remove the obligation to worship and obey the LORD.
- **New Testament connection:** Galatians 3:10–14 explains how Christ redeems His people from the curse of the law.

### Covenant with the Gibeonites — An Oath That Must Be Honored
- **In Joshua:** Joshua 9:3–27; 10:1–15.
- **Significance:** Israel must honor its sworn agreement even though it was obtained by deception.
- **Later reference:** 2 Samuel 21:1–6 shows the seriousness of violating this oath.

### Covenant Renewal at Shechem
- **In Joshua:** Joshua 24:1–28.
- **Significance:** Joshua calls Israel to reject idols and serve the LORD alone; the people publicly affirm their obligation.

### Davidic and New Covenants
- **Status:** Neither is formally established in Joshua. The book prepares the historical setting for the Davidic kingship and exposes Israel's ongoing need for deeper covenant faithfulness.

## Major Themes

- **God's covenant faithfulness:** His promises to the patriarchs stand firm.
- **Land and inheritance:** Canaan is a concrete covenant gift and anticipates a greater inheritance.
- **Courageous obedience:** Joshua's strength comes from God's presence and Word.
- **Holiness and judgment:** Achan's sin demonstrates the seriousness of covenant unfaithfulness.
- **Faith and mercy:** Rahab, a Canaanite, is welcomed and spared.
- **Rest:** Israel receives rest from major enemies, though the biblical promise of final rest extends beyond Joshua.
- **Worship and covenant loyalty:** Israel must choose whom it will serve.

## Key Redemptive Texts

| Passage | Redemptive significance |
|---|---|
| **Joshua 1:5–9** | God's presence with His appointed leader anticipates Christ's enduring presence with His people (Matthew 28:20). |
| **Joshua 2:8–14; 6:22–25** | Rahab's faith and rescue display mercy to a Gentile and anticipate the inclusion of the nations (Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25). |
| **Joshua 5:13–15** | The holy commander of the LORD's army underscores that victory belongs to God; identification of the figure as a preincarnate appearance of Christ is interpretive, not explicit. |
| **Joshua 20:1–9** | Cities of refuge offer a thematic picture of protection; compare the refuge believers find in God's promises (Hebrews 6:18). |
| **Joshua 21:43–45** | God's faithful gift of land and rest points toward the fuller rest discussed in Hebrews 4:8–11. |
| **Joshua 24:14–15** | The call to exclusive covenant allegiance anticipates the wholehearted discipleship demanded by Christ (Matthew 6:24). |

## Christ-Centered Typology

### Joshua and Jesus
Joshua's Hebrew name (*Yehoshua*) and Jesus' name (*Yeshua*) are related and convey the idea that the LORD saves. Joshua leads Israel into the land; Jesus brings His people into the greater salvation and rest of the new covenant (Matthew 1:21; Hebrews 4:8–11). **Joshua is a type, not an equal, of Christ.**

### Rahab and the inclusion of the nations
Rahab is a Gentile who believes the LORD and is spared from judgment (Joshua 2; 6). She appears in Jesus' genealogy (Matthew 1:5), showing the reach of God's redemptive purposes beyond Israel.

### The promised inheritance and final rest
Canaan is a genuine historical fulfillment of God's promise to Israel (Joshua 21:43–45). Hebrews 4:8–11 explicitly teaches that a greater rest remains for God's people.

### Judgment and salvation
Jericho's fall and Rahab's rescue place divine judgment and mercy side by side. The gospel likewise reveals God's justice and His saving mercy in Christ (Romans 3:25–26); this is a theological parallel, not a direct prediction in Joshua.

## Key Passages for Study

- **Joshua 1:7–9:** Courage rooted in God's Word and presence.
- **Joshua 2:9–11:** Rahab confesses the LORD's supremacy.
- **Joshua 5:13–15:** Holy ground and the LORD's army.
- **Joshua 7:10–13:** The seriousness of hidden sin.
- **Joshua 21:43–45:** God's promises fulfilled.
- **Joshua 23:14–16:** Blessings and warnings of covenant faithfulness.
- **Joshua 24:14–15:** “Choose this day whom you will serve.”

## Summary: What Joshua Reveals About God

The book of Joshua reveals the LORD as the sovereign, holy, just, merciful, and unwaveringly faithful God who keeps His covenant promises across generations. He grants Israel the land promised to Abraham, brings His people through the Jordan, defeats their enemies, and provides an inheritance they could never have won by strength alone. Yet His holiness demands obedience, and Israel's failures reveal the need for a greater salvation than military conquest or possession of land. Through Rahab's deliverance, Joshua's leadership, and the gift of rest, the book anticipates Jesus Christ, the greater Joshua, who saves His people from their sins and brings them into the lasting rest of God.
